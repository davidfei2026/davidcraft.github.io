HUMAN HISTORY

🏛️ Human History

A chronological journey through major developments in human civilization.

1. Early Humans

Human evolution occurred over a very long period. Homo sapiens evolved in Africa and eventually spread into many parts of the world.

Key idea: Human history is shaped by both environmental conditions and decisions made by individuals and societies.

2. The Agricultural Revolution

Beginning roughly 12,000 years ago in several regions, people increasingly domesticated plants and animals.

  • Permanent settlements became more common.
  • Food production increased in many regions.
  • Population sizes grew.
  • New forms of social organization developed.

3. First Cities and States

Large settlements emerged in places such as Mesopotamia, the Nile Valley, the Indus Valley and ancient China.

Governments, taxation, writing, specialized labor and long-distance trade became increasingly important.

4. Ancient Civilizations

  • Mesopotamia
  • Ancient Egypt
  • Indus Valley civilizations
  • Ancient China
  • Ancient Greece
  • Ancient Rome
  • Mesoamerican civilizations
  • Andean civilizations

5. Medieval Worlds

The medieval period saw powerful kingdoms and empires, growing cities, trade networks and major intellectual traditions.

Important developments occurred throughout Europe, Africa, Asia, the Islamic world and the Americas.

6. Global Trade Networks

Silk Roads, Indian Ocean trade and trans-Saharan trade connected distant societies through commerce and cultural exchange.

7. The Age of Exploration

From the late 1400s onward, European maritime voyages connected the Americas, Europe, Africa and Asia more directly.

These connections produced trade and exchange, but also colonization, exploitation, warfare and the spread of diseases.

8. Industrial Revolution

Industrialization transformed manufacturing, transportation, energy use, cities and labor.

  • Steam engines
  • Factories
  • Railroads
  • Coal
  • Later electricity and petroleum

9. Nationalism & Nation-States

During the 1800s, nationalism became an important political force. States such as Germany and Italy unified during this period.

10. The 20th Century

The twentieth century included two world wars, revolutions, decolonization, the Cold War, rapid technological change and major social transformations.

WARS & CONFLICTS

⚔️ Wars & Conflicts

Study major conflicts and the political, economic and social conditions surrounding them.

How historians study wars

Historians examine causes, decisions, military strategy, technology, geography, civilian experiences, economic resources and the aftermath of conflicts.

1618–1648

Thirty Years' War

A major European conflict that combined religious divisions, dynastic rivalries and competition among European states. Fighting devastated parts of Central Europe and changed the political balance of the continent.

1775–1783

American Revolutionary War

The thirteen American colonies fought Great Britain for independence. The conflict developed from disputes over taxation, representation and imperial authority, and ended with British recognition of American independence in the Treaty of Paris.

1792–1815

French Revolutionary & Napoleonic Wars

A series of wars connected to the French Revolution and the rise of Napoleon Bonaparte. French armies fought coalitions of European powers, and the conflicts redrew borders, spread political ideas and eventually ended with Napoleon's defeat in 1815.

1853–1856

Crimean War

A war centered on competition around the Black Sea and the declining Ottoman Empire. Britain and France supported the Ottoman Empire against Russia, and the conflict exposed weaknesses in military organization and logistics while influencing later reforms.

1861–1865

American Civil War

A U.S. civil war between the Union and the Confederacy. Slavery was central to the conflict, alongside disputes over secession, federal authority and the future of the country. The Union victory preserved the United States and led to the abolition of slavery.

1914–1918

World War I

A global conflict triggered by the July Crisis after the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand. Alliance commitments, mobilization, nationalism and imperial rivalry helped turn a regional crisis into a prolonged war involving Europe, the Middle East, Africa and other regions.

1939–1945

World War II

A worldwide conflict fought across multiple theaters after Germany invaded Poland in 1939 and later expanded across Europe, North Africa and Asia-Pacific. The war involved the Allied and Axis powers and resulted in enormous military and civilian losses, the Holocaust, major territorial changes and the creation of the United Nations.

1947–1991

Cold War

A prolonged geopolitical rivalry between the United States and Soviet Union. It involved competing political and economic systems, military alliances, nuclear deterrence, intelligence competition, technological rivalry and conflicts in which the superpowers supported opposing sides.

1950–1953

Korean War

A war on the Korean Peninsula that began when North Korean forces crossed the 38th parallel. The conflict became an international Cold War confrontation involving United Nations forces led largely by the United States and Chinese forces supporting North Korea. An armistice ended active fighting in 1953 without a formal peace treaty.

1955–1975

Vietnam War

A prolonged conflict in Vietnam involving North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces against South Vietnam and, especially during the later stages, substantial U.S. military involvement. It was shaped by Vietnamese political struggles, decolonization and Cold War rivalry. The conflict ended with the fall of Saigon in 1975 and the reunification of Vietnam.

1914–1918

🌍 World War I

The First World War transformed Europe and international politics.

Long-term causes

  • Nationalism
  • Military competition
  • Alliance systems
  • Imperial competition
  • Political tensions

The assassination at Sarajevo

On June 28, 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary was assassinated in Sarajevo.

The resulting diplomatic crisis escalated into a wider European war.

The major alliances

Group Important members
Central Powers Germany, Austria-Hungary, Ottoman Empire, Bulgaria
Allied Powers France, Britain, Russia, Italy, United States and others

Trench warfare

On the Western Front, opposing armies constructed extensive fortifications. Machine guns, artillery and defensive positions made attacks extremely costly.

New technology

  • Aircraft
  • Tanks
  • Machine guns
  • Submarines
  • Heavy artillery
  • Chemical weapons

The United States enters the war

The United States entered the war in 1917. American involvement added significant economic and military resources to the Allied side.

1918 and the armistice

The German Spring Offensive failed to achieve its objectives. The Allies then launched a series of successful offensives.

An armistice took effect on November 11, 1918.

Aftermath

The war caused enormous casualties and political change. The Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman, German and Russian empires were profoundly transformed or collapsed.

1939–1945

🗺️ World War II

The largest global conflict of the twentieth century.

Europe after World War I

The postwar period included political instability, economic crises, territorial disputes and competing ideologies.

🇩🇪 Nazi Germany

Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party established a dictatorship in Germany in 1933.

The Nazi regime destroyed democratic institutions, persecuted political opponents, promoted antisemitism and racist ideology, and pursued territorial expansion.

Historical context: Nazi ideology was based on racism, antisemitism and dictatorship. The regime's policies led to persecution, war and mass murder. This section is educational history, not an endorsement of the ideology.

Expansion before the war

  • Remilitarization of the Rhineland — 1936
  • Anschluss with Austria — 1938
  • Munich Agreement — 1938
  • Occupation of the Czech lands — 1939
  • Invasion of Poland — 1939

September 1, 1939

Germany invaded Poland. Britain and France declared war on Germany, beginning World War II in Europe.

1940 — Western Europe

Germany defeated France and occupied large parts of Western Europe. Britain continued the war after the evacuation from Dunkirk.

1941 — The war becomes global

Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941. In December, Japan attacked Pearl Harbor, bringing the United States directly into the war.

✡️ The Holocaust

The Holocaust was the systematic persecution and murder of approximately six million Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators.

Millions of other people were also persecuted and murdered, including Roma, disabled people, political opponents and Soviet prisoners of war.

Major turning points

  • Battle of Britain
  • Operation Barbarossa
  • Pearl Harbor
  • Battle of Midway
  • Battle of Stalingrad
  • Second Battle of El Alamein
  • D-Day / Normandy invasion
  • Soviet advances into Eastern Europe

1944 — D-Day

On June 6, 1944, Allied forces landed in Normandy. The operation opened a major Western Allied front in Europe.

1945 — End of the European war

Soviet forces reached Berlin while Western Allied forces advanced from the west. Nazi Germany surrendered in May 1945.

Japan and the end of World War II

The war in the Pacific continued after Germany's surrender. Japan surrendered in August 1945, ending World War II.

The aftermath

  • United Nations founded in 1945
  • Europe devastated by war
  • Germany divided into occupation zones
  • United States and Soviet Union emerged as superpowers
  • Cold War began
  • Decolonization accelerated
1947–1991

☢️ The Cold War

A decades-long global rivalry between the United States and Soviet Union and their respective allies.

What was the Cold War?

The Cold War was not a single continuous battlefield war between the two superpowers. Instead, it was a broad competition over political systems, military power, economics, technology and global influence. The United States generally promoted liberal democracy and a market-oriented economy, while the Soviet Union led a communist political and economic bloc.

Military alliances

NATO was established in 1949, while the Soviet-led Warsaw Pact was created in 1955. Both alliances were designed around collective security and became major parts of the military division of Europe.

Nuclear deterrence

Both superpowers developed large nuclear arsenals. The possibility that a nuclear exchange could cause catastrophic destruction encouraged leaders to avoid direct war while still competing intensely through diplomacy, arms development and proxy conflicts.

Major Cold War crises

  • Berlin Blockade and Airlift — 1948–1949
  • Korean War — 1950–1953
  • Cuban Missile Crisis — 1962
  • Vietnam War — major U.S. involvement during the 1960s and early 1970s
  • Soviet invasion of Afghanistan — 1979
  • Fall of the Berlin Wall — 1989
  • Dissolution of the Soviet Union — 1991

How did it end?

During the 1980s, reforms in the Soviet Union, political changes in Eastern Europe and growing economic pressures weakened the Soviet system. Communist governments across Eastern Europe fell in 1989, the Berlin Wall opened, and the Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991.

1950–1953

🇰🇷 Korean War

A major Cold War conflict that divided the Korean Peninsula and drew in international forces.

Background

After World War II, Korea was divided near the 38th parallel into Soviet- and U.S.-occupied zones. Two separate governments emerged in 1948: North Korea and South Korea. Both claimed legitimacy over the entire peninsula.

The war begins

On June 25, 1950, North Korean forces crossed the 38th parallel and advanced south. South Korean and United Nations forces, with the United States providing the largest contingent, attempted to stop the advance.

International intervention

The United Nations authorized a multinational force. After UN forces pushed north, China entered the war in late 1950 on North Korea's side. Fighting then moved back and forth across the peninsula before settling near the original dividing line.

Armistice and legacy

An armistice was signed on July 27, 1953. It created a Demilitarized Zone, or DMZ, near the fighting line. The armistice stopped large-scale fighting but did not create a permanent peace treaty, so the peninsula remains technically in a state of war.

1955–1975

🇻🇳 Vietnam War

A long conflict shaped by Vietnamese political struggles, decolonization and Cold War rivalry.

Historical background

Vietnam had been part of French Indochina. After World War II, Vietnamese independence forces fought France. The French defeat at Dien Bien Phu in 1954 was followed by the Geneva Accords, which temporarily divided Vietnam near the 17th parallel.

North and South Vietnam

North Vietnam, led by the communist government of Ho Chi Minh, sought reunification. South Vietnam had a separate government supported by the United States and other anti-communist partners. Political and military conflict intensified during the 1950s and 1960s.

U.S. involvement

The United States gradually expanded its military role, sending large numbers of troops and conducting extensive air operations. The war also involved the Viet Cong, North Vietnamese forces and South Vietnamese forces. The conflict caused major disruption and loss of life throughout Vietnam and neighboring countries.

Ending of the war

The Paris Peace Accords were signed in 1973, after which U.S. combat forces withdrew. Fighting between North and South continued. In April 1975, North Vietnamese forces captured Saigon, and Vietnam was later reunified under the communist government.

Historical significance

The war had lasting effects on Vietnam, the United States and Southeast Asia. It influenced U.S. foreign policy, public opinion, military strategy and debates over government credibility, while Vietnam faced enormous reconstruction challenges.

MODERN ECONOMICS • LIFE SKILLS

💰 Modern Economics

Understand how economies work, how money moves, and the financial concepts people encounter in everyday adult life.

📘 What Economics Is

Economics is the study of how people, businesses, governments and societies make choices when resources are limited. It helps explain why prices change, why businesses compete, why governments collect taxes, why unemployment rises and falls, and how decisions in one part of an economy can affect people somewhere else. Economics is not simply about money: it also examines time, labor, natural resources, technology, trade and the choices people make about competing goals.

A useful way to think about economics is to ask three questions: What resources are available? What choices are being made? What are the costs and benefits of those choices? Learning these ideas can help you understand news reports, government policies, business decisions and ordinary household decisions.

⚖️ Scarcity

Scarcity means that resources are limited compared with the many things people want or need. A household has a limited amount of income and time. A business has limited workers, equipment and materials. A government also faces limits on its available resources. Because resources are scarce, every society has to make choices about what to produce, how to produce it and who receives the resulting goods and services.

🔀 Opportunity Cost

Opportunity cost is the next-best alternative given up when a choice is made. If someone spends an hour studying, the opportunity cost could be the other useful activity they could have done during that hour. Understanding opportunity cost is useful because a decision can have a cost even when no money changes hands. Businesses, governments and families all use this idea when comparing different options.

📦 Supply

Supply describes how much of a good or service producers are willing and able to sell at different prices. When producing something becomes more expensive, difficult or risky, businesses may have less incentive to supply it. Technology, wages, raw-material costs, taxes, regulations and expectations about future conditions can all influence supply. Supply is one part of the interaction that helps determine market prices.

🛒 Demand

Demand describes how much of a good or service consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices. Demand can change because of income, preferences, population, expectations, the prices of related products and other circumstances. A popular product may experience strong demand, while demand for another product may fall when consumers find alternatives or when their budgets become tighter.

📈 GDP

Gross Domestic Product, or GDP, measures the market value of final goods and services produced within an economy during a period. Economists use GDP as one important indicator of economic activity and the size of an economy. GDP can rise because an economy produces more goods and services, because prices increase, or for other reasons, so it should not be treated as a complete measure of people's well-being. GDP also does not by itself show how income is distributed.

💵 Inflation

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services. When inflation occurs, a unit of currency generally buys fewer goods and services than before. Inflation can have many causes, including changes in demand, production costs and the overall supply of money and credit. Economists usually look at broad price indexes rather than a single product because individual prices can rise or fall for many different reasons.

👷 Unemployment

Unemployment measures people who are without jobs and are actively seeking and available for work. Economists distinguish among different types of unemployment, including temporary unemployment while people change jobs, unemployment connected to changes in industries and skills, and unemployment associated with economic downturns. The unemployment rate is important, but it does not capture every aspect of the labor market or every person's experience with work.

🏦 Interest Rates

Interest is the cost of borrowing money or the return earned from lending or saving. Interest rates affect the cost of loans and can influence decisions by households, businesses and governments. Higher rates can make borrowing more expensive and can encourage saving, while lower rates can make borrowing less expensive. The actual effect depends on the type of loan or account, its terms, fees, inflation and other conditions.

🏛️ Economic Systems

Economic systems describe how societies organize production, ownership and decision-making. Market economies rely heavily on voluntary exchange and price signals. Command economies place more production and allocation decisions under centralized authority. Mixed economies combine market activity with government programs, laws and regulation. In practice, countries use different combinations of these approaches, and their systems can change over time.

  • Market economies: prices and production are influenced substantially by buyers and sellers.
  • Command economies: centralized authorities make major decisions about production and allocation.
  • Mixed economies: markets operate alongside government services, taxation and regulation.

👔 Jobs, Wages & Paychecks

For many adults, employment is the main source of income. A job offer may list a gross wage or salary, but the amount that reaches a worker's bank account can be lower because of taxes and other deductions. Benefits such as health coverage, paid time off or retirement contributions can also affect the overall value of a job. When comparing employment, it is useful to understand the difference between gross pay, take-home pay, hourly pay, salary, overtime rules and employer-provided benefits.

Labor markets are influenced by workers' skills, employer demand, productivity, education, location, working conditions and broader economic conditions. Wages can differ between occupations and regions, and they can change as demand for particular skills changes.

🧾 Taxes

Taxes are payments collected by governments to help fund public services and programs. Common examples include income taxes, sales taxes and property taxes, although the exact tax system depends on the country and jurisdiction. A paycheck may have taxes withheld before the worker receives it. Some taxes are paid directly by individuals, while others are collected by businesses and passed to the government.

A basic adult financial skill is learning to read important tax documents, keep records, understand which taxes apply to a situation, and meet filing or payment deadlines. Tax rules can be complicated and change over time, so people should use current official information or qualified professional help when they need specific tax guidance.

🧮 Budgeting & Cash Flow

A budget is a plan for how income will be used. A simple budget separates money coming in from regular and irregular expenses. Essential expenses can include housing, food, utilities, transportation and required payments, while other spending may be optional. Tracking actual spending can reveal patterns that are difficult to notice when expenses are considered one purchase at a time.

Cash flow matters because the timing of income and bills can be important even when total income over a month seems sufficient. A person may need to plan for annual or irregular expenses rather than treating them as surprises. The goal of budgeting is to understand choices and tradeoffs, not to make every person's spending look the same.

🏦 Banking

Banks and credit unions provide services such as checking accounts, savings accounts, payments and loans. Adults should understand account fees, minimum-balance requirements, transaction limits, interest rates and how to review statements. Keeping account information secure is also important: strong passwords, multi-factor authentication and careful handling of unexpected messages can reduce the risk of account takeover.

💳 Credit & Credit Scores

Credit allows someone to borrow money or obtain goods and services with an agreement to pay later. A credit card is not free money: purchases create an obligation to repay according to the account terms, and interest may apply when a balance is carried. Loans can also include interest, fees and other costs.

Credit reports contain information about borrowing and repayment history, while credit scores are numerical measures calculated using information in credit reports. Different scoring systems can use different methods. Adults should review important credit information, understand the terms of borrowing, and avoid taking on payments they cannot reasonably afford.

🏠 Housing & Major Purchases

Housing is often one of the largest expenses in an adult household. Renting may involve rent, deposits, utilities, insurance and other costs. Buying a home can involve a down payment, a mortgage, interest, taxes, insurance, maintenance and transaction costs. The advertised price of a major purchase is therefore not always the same as its total long-term cost.

Before signing an important agreement, adults should read the terms carefully, understand recurring costs and know what happens if circumstances change. Contracts can create legal and financial obligations, so unclear terms should be investigated before signing.

🛡️ Insurance

Insurance is a way of transferring certain financial risks to an insurer in exchange for premiums. Depending on the policy, insurance can help cover specified losses involving health, vehicles, homes, renters' property or other risks. Important terms include the premium, deductible, coverage limits, exclusions and claims process.

Insurance does not cover every possible event. Adults should understand what a policy actually covers rather than assuming that a policy protects against every loss. Comparing policies requires looking at both price and coverage details.

🌱 Saving & Emergency Funds

Saving means setting aside part of current income for future needs or goals. An emergency fund is money reserved for unexpected expenses such as repairs, urgent travel or a temporary loss of income. The appropriate amount depends on a person's circumstances, income stability and expenses. Keeping emergency savings accessible can make it easier to handle surprises without immediately relying on expensive borrowing.

🕰️ Retirement & Long-Term Planning

Retirement planning involves preparing for a period when a person may no longer receive regular employment income. Long-term planning can involve workplace retirement programs, individual accounts, savings and other sources of future income. The earlier money is saved, the more time there may be for growth, but outcomes depend on contributions, fees, investment performance, inflation and many other factors.

A key concept is compound growth: money that remains invested or saved can potentially earn returns, and those returns can themselves contribute to future growth. Because financial products and tax rules vary, adults should learn the terms and risks of an account before using it.

📊 Investing: Risk & Return

Investing means putting money into assets with the expectation that they may produce a return. Different assets can have different levels of risk, possible returns, fees and time horizons. Diversification means spreading money among different investments rather than relying entirely on one asset. It can reduce the effect of a poor result from one investment, although it cannot eliminate investment risk.

A basic rule of financial literacy is that higher potential returns generally come with greater uncertainty or risk. Past performance does not guarantee future results. Adults should understand what they are buying, what fees apply, how easily they can access the money, and what could cause its value to fall.

🌎 Trade & Globalization

Countries trade because different places have different resources, skills, technologies, climates and production costs. Comparative advantage describes how specializing in activities with lower opportunity costs can create gains from trade. International trade can increase access to products and markets, while also creating adjustment costs for industries and workers affected by competition or changes in global demand.

Global supply chains connect businesses across countries. A disruption in transportation, energy, raw materials or manufacturing in one region can therefore affect prices and availability somewhere else.

🏛️ Central Banks & Monetary Policy

Central banks are institutions responsible for important parts of a country's monetary and financial system. Depending on the country, a central bank may influence short-term interest rates, provide banking-system services and work toward goals such as price stability and sustainable economic conditions. Monetary policy can affect borrowing, saving, spending and inflation, but it does not control every part of the economy.

🏛️ Government Spending & Fiscal Policy

Fiscal policy refers to government decisions about taxation and spending. Governments may spend money on areas such as infrastructure, education, public safety, health programs and other services. They may also collect taxes and borrow money. During economic downturns, governments can sometimes change spending or taxes in an effort to support economic activity, while fiscal decisions can also create long-term budget pressures.

🚨 Financial Scams & Consumer Protection

Financial scams often use urgency, fear, promises of easy money or requests for sensitive information. Common warning signs include pressure to act immediately, unexpected requests for passwords or account codes, demands for unusual payments, and promises of guaranteed profits. Adults should verify requests independently instead of trusting the contact information provided in a suspicious message.

Before sending money or signing an agreement, check who you are dealing with, read the terms, and be cautious when someone says you must keep the transaction secret. When something seems too good to be true, slowing down is often safer than acting immediately.

🧠 What Adults Should Know About Money

Adult financial life combines many of the concepts above. A person may receive income, pay taxes, manage a bank account, use credit, pay for housing and insurance, save for emergencies, plan for long-term goals and make major purchases. Each decision involves tradeoffs. A strong foundation in financial literacy helps people understand those tradeoffs and ask better questions.

  • Understand the difference between gross income and take-home income.
  • Know what recurring expenses and irregular expenses you have.
  • Read the terms before agreeing to a loan, subscription, lease or other contract.
  • Understand interest, fees, minimum payments and total repayment costs before borrowing.
  • Keep important financial records organized and protect account credentials.
  • Build savings for expected and unexpected expenses when possible.
  • Understand the risks, fees and time horizon of investments before using them.
  • Be cautious with unsolicited financial offers and promises of guaranteed returns.
  • Use current official information when dealing with taxes, government programs or legal requirements.

📚 Economics in the Real World

Economic concepts appear in everyday headlines: inflation affects purchasing power, interest rates affect borrowing conditions, unemployment affects the labor market, GDP provides information about economic activity, and international trade affects businesses and consumers. No single statistic tells the whole story. Good economic reasoning means looking at several pieces of evidence, asking what a measurement actually represents, and recognizing uncertainty.

Economics can also help explain disagreements about public policy. Two people may agree about a goal but disagree about which policy will achieve it, who should pay for it, what unintended effects could occur, or how the benefits and costs should be distributed. Studying economics does not require agreeing with one particular political or economic viewpoint; it provides tools for evaluating claims and understanding tradeoffs.

✍️ WRITING

Writing & Language Arts

Build clear, organized writing skills for school, research, creative work, and everyday communication.

Lesson 1 — Sentences and Paragraphs

A strong sentence communicates a complete idea. A strong paragraph usually develops one main idea through a topic sentence, supporting details, explanations, and a closing or transition. Good writing is clear before it is complicated.

Practice: Read each paragraph and ask: What is its main idea? Does every sentence help develop it?

Lesson 2 — Grammar and Punctuation

Grammar describes how words and sentences are structured. Learn subject-verb agreement, verb tense, pronouns, conjunctions, sentence boundaries, capitalization, commas, apostrophes, and quotation marks. Editing should make meaning easier to understand.

Lesson 3 — Essays and Evidence

An essay normally has a clear purpose, an introduction, organized body paragraphs, evidence or examples, and a conclusion. Evidence is most useful when the writer explains how it supports the point being made.

Lesson 4 — Revision and Research

Revision is more than fixing spelling. First examine ideas, organization, audience, evidence, and clarity. Then proofread grammar and punctuation. When researching, keep track of sources so information can be credited accurately.

➗ MATHEMATICS

Mathematics

Learn mathematical reasoning from numbers and operations through algebra, geometry, statistics, probability, and practical problem solving.

Lesson 1 — Fractions, Decimals and Percentages

Fractions represent parts or ratios, decimals represent values in place-value notation, and percentages express a quantity per hundred. Converting between these forms helps with measurements, discounts, taxes, rates, and data.

Example: 0.75 = 75/100 = 3/4 = 75%.

Lesson 2 — Ratios and Proportions

Ratios compare quantities and proportions describe equivalent relationships. These ideas appear in maps, recipes, scale drawings, speed, unit rates, and many real-world comparisons.

Lesson 3 — Algebra

Algebra uses variables to represent unknown or changing quantities. An equation states that two expressions have equal value. Solve equations by making equivalent changes to both sides while keeping track of what the variable represents.

Example: x + 7 = 12, so subtract 7 from both sides: x = 5.

Lesson 4 — Geometry

Geometry studies shapes, distance, angles, area, surface area, and volume. Diagrams help identify known measurements and the formula that matches the problem.

Lesson 5 — Statistics and Probability

Statistics summarizes data with measures and graphs. Probability describes likelihood under a defined model or set of possible outcomes. Always consider sample size, representation, and whether a graph could create a misleading impression.

🏛️ SOCIAL STUDIES

Social Studies

Explore civics, government, communities, culture, geography, citizenship, and historical thinking.

Lesson 1 — Civics and Citizenship

Civics studies public institutions and the ways people participate in community and government. Citizenship can involve rights, responsibilities, informed participation, public discussion, and understanding how decisions are made.

Lesson 2 — Government and Institutions

Governments create systems for making rules, providing services, resolving disputes, and managing public resources. Political systems differ, so study constitutions or basic laws, institutions, elections or leadership selection, courts, and limits on authority.

Lesson 3 — Culture and Society

Societies include many communities, languages, traditions, institutions, and social practices. Culture changes through migration, technology, economic shifts, and contact between communities. Avoid assuming everyone in a culture has identical experiences or opinions.

Lesson 4 — Human Geography

Physical geography affects settlement, while people also transform environments through farming, cities, transportation, industry, and infrastructure. Population density, resources, climate, trade routes, and political boundaries shape human geography.

Lesson 5 — Historical Thinking

Historical thinking asks how evidence supports conclusions about the past. Primary sources come from the period being studied; secondary sources interpret evidence. Context, perspective, chronology, cause and effect, continuity, and change are all important.

💻 TECHNOLOGY

Technology & Computer Science

Understand computers, coding, networks, data, cybersecurity, AI, and responsible digital citizenship.

Lesson 1 — How Computers Work

Computers receive input, process information, store data, and produce output. Hardware includes physical components such as processors, memory, storage, displays, and network equipment. Software consists of instructions and data.

Lesson 2 — Coding and Algorithms

Programming turns problem-solving ideas into instructions. An algorithm is a defined procedure for completing a task. Variables store values, conditionals choose paths, and loops repeat operations.

Pattern: Input → process → condition → action → output.

Lesson 3 — Internet and Networks

The internet is a network of networks using standardized protocols. Websites commonly involve browsers, servers, domain names, and transferred data. HTML structures a page, CSS controls presentation, and JavaScript can provide interactive behavior.

Lesson 4 — Data

Text, images, audio, and video can all be represented as digital data. Databases organize information so software can store, retrieve, and update it. Inaccurate or incomplete data can produce poor conclusions.

Lesson 5 — Cybersecurity and Privacy

Cybersecurity protects devices, accounts, networks, and information from unauthorized access, disruption, or misuse. Useful habits include strong unique passwords, security updates, careful handling of suspicious messages, and protecting sensitive information.

Lesson 6 — AI and Digital Citizenship

AI systems can recognize patterns, generate content, make predictions, and assist with decisions. Outputs can still be wrong or incomplete, so important information should be checked. Digital citizenship includes respecting others, protecting privacy, checking information, and thinking about consequences.
